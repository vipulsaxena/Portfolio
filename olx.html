The starting point

Sellers were dropping out of the buy-package flow — especially at category selection.

Identified friction 01

Extreme drop-off at category selection

Diagnosis: Almost 80% of B2C and 90% of C2C Sellers abandoned the buy-package flow at the category-selection step — before they could purchase an Ad package.

Identified friction 02

Category hierarchy didn’t match seller mental models

Diagnosis: Long L2+ lists, non-clickable sub-category bullets, and weak links between categories and package pricing made advance package buying feel opaque.

Synthesis

We analysed the 24 identified limitations

Post qualitative research, findings were clustered into 24 limitations. With: Research Lead, Lead PM, HOD, myself & 2 Jr. Designers.

Sellers can't relate to what personalisation does to their AdsThe personalised-page option wasn't transparent or attractive enough to users.
Statistics feel like a free feature — why pay for it?Sellers assume Ad statistics are always free, so the benefit of paying is unclear.
No freedom to decide when to use Pay-if-you-sellYou first have to use up the pool of free ads to use STR — and Sellers often don't know this.
Hard to find the exact category when buying Ad packagesSellers don't find the category-selection process user-friendly. OpScore 13.12 — the bet.
Sellers don't see the direct results of paying for promotionsPromotion lifts views, but that didn't clearly translate into sales versus re-posting.
Package price is often inadequate to item priceFor cheap items the package price is close to the item's value — the sale is no longer profitable.

Prioritisation

Quantification to prioritisation

I surveyed Sellers on the importance & satisfaction of each problem area, then used Opportunity Scoring to generate an OpScore. Category selection was a top opportunity — the bet we chose.

OpScore = Importance + max(Importance − Satisfaction, 0)
03
Drop-offUX audit

The category-selection problem

Almost 80% of B2C and 90% of C2C Sellers drop at the category-selection page of the buy-package flow.

“I like the option to buy an Ad package in advance, but it gets tricky to find the same category in which I post multiple ads.”

Funnel data showed only 20.55% of users reaching package selected — with category-selection flagged at OpScore 13.12 as the highest-opportunity friction point in the buy-package journey.

04
ExploreTest

Desktop-first exploration & validation

More than 60% of Ad packages are purchased via the OLX website — so we explored on desktop, narrowed to two concepts, and tested with 10 users.

Explore

Designed 3 possible directions for desktop

#1 Hypothesis

Real-time offer view

Users would appreciate if they can simultaneously browse Ad packages while choosing the category in which they'd like to post ads, making the overall process dynamic.

Offers are shown based on availability. Categories are seen upfront on the offer view.

#2 Hypothesis

Multi-level menu

A linear selection process should reduce cognitive load, along with the multi-level menu that should ease the selection process.

Minimum selection to see offers would be L2 selection. Selected category is marked with a '✔' at summary & offer page.

#3 Hypothesis

Multi-selection view

OLX only offers packages in pre-set bundled categories; letting users multi-select category bundles could enable advance planning.

Letting users multi-select categories across various L1 would require change in business logic (high effort), and so in the current state only multiple bundles can be selected.

Two directions, head to head

Design critique to narrow it down to 2 ideas — 9 P&S designers, 4 PMs across pillars, the Engineering team, the P&S HOD and the Data team.

Test

We tested 2 concepts for usability with 10 users

Moderated sessions compared Variant A (real-time offer view) against Variant B (multi-level menu) — each insight paired with the takeaway it drove.

A 4/8 struggled to associate dropdown categories with the dynamic offer. Task time was 15% lower than Production.
→
Primary/secondary/tertiary categories got associated with filters — adding cognitive load and failing the hypothesis.
B 7/8 were satisfied with the multi-level menu and appreciated the summary view. Task time was 28% higher than Production.
→
Users found categories with ease; one suggested a shortcut to repeat the selection.
A Only 2/8 found the filter view easier to compare — and they ignored the 'see-more' CTA while comparing packages.
→
Most users expect offers to carry as much upfront information as possible.
B With upfront category tags, 5/8 intuitively named categories as a variable for why price differs per package.
→
Users don't always tap see-more — a long list of category tags can also add cognitive load.
